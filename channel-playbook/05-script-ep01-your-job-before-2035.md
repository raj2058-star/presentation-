## BEAT 1: What "exposed" means (0:45–1:45)

Start with the scary number. In January 2024, the International Monetary Fund looked at which jobs AI could affect. Their answer was almost 40% of all jobs worldwide. In advanced economies, about 60%. In emerging economies, about 40%. In low-income countries, about 26%.

`[ON SCREEN: Three bars filling: 60% / 40% / 26%, labeled "Advanced", "Emerging", "Low-income".]`

But here's the part that rarely makes the headline. The IMF split those exposed jobs roughly in half. In about half, AI is expected to *help* the person doing the job: making them faster, maybe more valuable. In the other half, AI could do enough of the work that employers need fewer people, or pay less. `[VERIFY: IMF wording on the "complement" vs "replace" split]`

`[ON SCREEN: The 60% bar splits into two colors. Amber: "AI helps the worker". Red: "AI reduces demand".]`

So *exposed* doesn't mean *replaced*. It means *changed*. And the whole question of the next ten years is which half your job lands in.

## BEAT 2: The two numbers everyone quotes (1:45–2:50)

The second forecast is the World Economic Forum's Future of Jobs Report, from January 2025. They surveyed more than a thousand of the world's biggest employers across 55 economies and asked what they expect to happen to their workforce by 2030.

`[ON SCREEN: A world map with 55 countries highlighted. Text: "1,000+ employers · 55 economies".]`

Their answer was 170 million new jobs created by 2030, and 92 million jobs displaced. That's a net gain of about 78 million jobs.

`[ON SCREEN: Two columns stacking up. Amber "+170M created", red "−92M displaced". Then they subtract to a single figure: "+78M net".]`

That sounds like good news, and in total it is. But a net gain is no comfort if you're in the 92 million. The people losing jobs and the people getting the new ones are often not the same people. So let's look at who's on each list.

## BEAT 3: The shrinking list (2:50–3:40)

The jobs the report expects to decline fastest are mostly `[READ FROM REPORT: the fastest-declining roles, e.g. clerical, cashier and data-entry roles]`.

`[ON SCREEN: A descending list of role names, each with a small red down-arrow. Source tag: "WEF Future of Jobs 2025".]`

Notice what they have in common. These are jobs where most of the day is the same task, done the same way, on a screen or at a counter. That's exactly the kind of work software has been absorbing for decades. AI just speeds it up.

## BEAT 4: The growing list, and the surprise (3:40–4:50)

Now the growing list. By *percentage*, the fastest-growing roles are what you'd expect: big data specialists, fintech engineers, and AI and machine learning specialists.

`[ON SCREEN: Three role cards with upward amber arrows.]`

But by *headcount*, by sheer number of new jobs, the biggest growth in the whole report isn't in tech at all. It's farmworkers, with around 35 million more by 2030.

`[ON SCREEN: A small tech-jobs bar next to a huge farmworkers bar towering over it. Text: "+35M farmworkers". Hold for a beat.]`

The rest of the biggest-growth list follows the same pattern: `[READ FROM REPORT: the other large-growth roles, e.g. delivery drivers, construction, care and nursing roles]`. These are jobs where you have to be physically there, with your hands, or with another person. The future of work isn't only coders. It's also people doing things a screen can't.

## BEAT 5: The twist: the job stays, the skills change (4:50–5:50)

So here's the strange part I mentioned at the start. For most people, the job title doesn't disappear. What changes is what's *inside* it.

The same WEF report found that employers expect nearly 40% of the skills workers use today to change by 2030. And 63% of employers said the skills gap is the biggest thing holding their business back. `[VERIFY: exact wording of both figures]`

`[ON SCREEN: A job title card stays fixed in the center while the skill tags orbiting it fade out and new ones fade in. Counter: "≈40% of skills change by 2030".]`

That's the real shift. It's not a robot sitting in your chair one morning. It's the same chair, the same title, and a different job.

## BEAT 6: The payoff: the 3-question test (5:50–7:50)

So, as promised, here's how to tell which half your job is in. Three questions. Answer them honestly about your actual day, not your job description.

`[ON SCREEN: The checklist from the turn returns. Each box ticks as it's asked.]`

**Question one: is most of your day the *task*, or *judgment about* the task?**
If most of your time is producing the same output, like filling in, sorting, transcribing or summarising, AI can do more of it. If most of your time is deciding what to do, for whom, and whether it's right, AI makes you faster. That's the IMF's two halves, applied to you.

`[ON SCREEN: A slider from "TASK" to "JUDGMENT". The marker moves as examples flash past.]`

**Question two: does your work need hands, presence, or another human in the room?**
Remember the farmworkers, the drivers, the carers. Physical, in-person work sits on the growing side of the report.

`[ON SCREEN: Icons of a hand, a location pin and two people. Each lights amber.]`

**Question three: when your work gets cheaper to do, does demand for it go up?**
This one is subtle. If AI makes your work twice as fast and the world simply wants twice as much of it, you're fine. If the world only ever needs the same amount, fewer people do it.

`[ON SCREEN: Two simple graphs. "Demand grows": the line rises. "Demand fixed": the line flat and the worker icons halving.]`

If you answered *judgment*, *yes*, and *yes*, your job is likely to be changed, not cut. If you answered *task*, *no* and *no*, now is the time to start moving toward the skills on the other side, while you still have time to choose.
